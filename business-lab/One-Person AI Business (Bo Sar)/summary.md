## The one-sentence version

Sell a **paid AI process audit** to a **single named niche** of established businesses, deliver it with **Claude plugins that hold your delivery process in files instead of employees**, and use the audit's map — with dollar figures pulled from the client's own team — as the entry point to everything you sell afterwards.

## The problem it solves

Building AI is no longer a moat. The owner's nephew can build the chatbot demo in a weekend, and every business owner has already received forty identical AI cold emails this week. Meanwhile demand is at an all-time high — the *majority* of the adoption curve has arrived, and they buy on proof and ROI, not on impressive demos.

**The gap in this market is trust, not awareness.** And not all proof closes it: a random chatbot project is not a case study. A case study is *"quoting took 20 hours a week, now it takes 3 — $28k/year — and here's the client saying it on camera."*

## The map

Every service business is three parts, and exactly **one** of them is your binding constraint at any moment:

| | job |
|---|---|
| **Traffic** | fill the funnel with opportunities |
| **System** | convert opportunities into appointments, contracts, cash |
| **Skill** | deliver so well you get paid, kept and referred |

Rule: **one traffic source, one conversion mechanism, one delivery channel.** Build the plumbing before you send water through it.

**The 2026 reframe:** the old way to scale was transferring skill into employees — slow, expensive, high turnover. The AI-first way is transferring skill into **files** (plugins and skills) that you copy for free. That is the entire reason a one-person version of this business exists now and didn't three years ago.

## 1 · Traffic

Four sources, in the order you can actually use them:

- **Outreach** — fine for your first conversations and reps; a treadmill, not a machine. Zero trust, price competition, and brutal response rates in 2026.
- **Content** — the best leads that exist (trust arrives before the call), but the algorithm decides your month, it takes years, and it's tied to your face.
- **Paid ads** — the only source where **volume responds to money on demand**. Once the dial works, your constraint moves to system and skill, which you can engineer.
- **Referrals** — cheapest, highest trust, and a *system* not luck: ask at the peak, ask with a specific list, pay 20% commission, price so the client gets ~5× value. But it's a multiplier, not a dial — useless at three clients.

**Order of operations:** outreach + inbound until the offer is proven and a few clients have paid → *then* paid, to scale a formula that already works. Discovering the formula with paid is too expensive.

**General traffic brings general leads.** Name your ICP in the traffic itself ("construction companies above $1M/year"). Similar problems → templated solution → productized → automatable delivery. This is the shift that turns gigs into a business.

**The Meta doctrine:** one campaign, one ad set, ~20 creatives, CBO, ~$100/day. Targeting = country only — no age, gender or interests, because the algorithm reallocates by itself and does it better. 95% of results come from creatives. You niche down through the **first line of the copy**, not the settings; wrong-fit traffic bouncing is a feature that teaches the pixel. Optimize for booked calls, with the Conversions API wired.

**Benchmark:** ~$85 per qualified booked call. $4,000/week ≈ 50 booked calls → close 20–30% of a $10k offer → a seven-figure business with a very small team.

## 2 · System — the funnel and the sales process

**Landing page:** deliberately boring tech (plain HTML — frameworks load slower and load time costs leads). VSL + testimonials + case studies, then qualifying questions, then the calendar. Nobody is forced to watch; the qualifying questions do the filtering.

**The trick almost nobody uses:** the booking flow captures **name and phone first**, creating a CRM record instantly. Everything else — qualifying questions, calendar — comes as separate steps. So when someone abandons halfway, you still have them, a notification fires, and you call **within 5–10 minutes**. Speed to lead is free.

**Operational habits that make the difference:**
- Confirmation call within 5 minutes of booking — it gets a spoken commitment, sends them a long-form video to close the trust gap before the call, and cuts no-shows.
- No-show → call immediately, **double dial**. One missed call reads as spam; a second right after reads as a real person. ~90% who pick up rebook.
- Rebooking: **never send a link.** Offer exactly two slots and book it for them. An open calendar is a decision; two slots is a coin flip.
- Selling into another country? A Twilio caller app dialing from local numbers is an afternoon build and makes the whole thing work from anywhere.

**Call one — goal is a scheduled call two, not a close.**
- You talk 20%, they talk 80%. Diagnose, don't pitch.
- **Dig for numbers.** "Takes forever" is not data. How many hours, who does it, what's their hourly cost. *Hours × rate = hidden cost*, and hidden cost is the foundation of the deal.
- **Tell them where AI shouldn't be used in their business.** Everyone else is hype; the realist wins the room instantly. Trust is built by what you refuse to sell.
- **Show the deliverable** — a demo audit with dummy data and real structure: process map, pain points tied to solutions, hidden-cost table quoting their own team, priority matrix, risk section. The prospect needs to see the thing to buy the thing.
- Name the price *while they're looking at it*, with the guarantee.
- Book call two live, and have them click the confirmation while you watch.

**Proposal within 15 minutes**, generated by a Claude skill that pulls the call transcript from the note-taker into the vault, checks the calendar, matches the tone of the existing email thread, and passes through a skeptical reviewer agent. Speed isn't the moat — the iterations that keep it from reading like AI slop are.

**Call two closes at ~80%** if they show. Terms: 50% upfront, 50% on delivery; first half clears before delivery starts. **The sale is a sale only when the money is in the bank.**

**The one objection:** "another agency will just build the automation for $6k." Answer: they'll automate one part that isn't built around everything else, so the process next to it starts leaking, then the next, until you pay someone to rip it out and rebuild. You wouldn't build a house without a foundation. *Pattern:* the ones pushing hardest to skip the audit are almost always 5–10 person teams whose owner is sure he knows everything. Bigger decision makers never argue. Treat it as a qualification signal.

**With zero case studies: buy your proof.** Price lower — but never *announce* that you're new. **$3,000 is the floor** (a suspiciously cheap audit is a red flag to a $1M business); anchor at $5k and let it come down. You're not making money yet; you're collecting before-and-after stories. Ask for the testimonial at the peak, recorded as an interview you control. **Variation worth stealing:** bundle the first automation from the priority matrix into the audit price, so the deliverable becomes a before-and-after instead of paper.

## 3 · Skill — delivering the audit

**Raw material is interviews** — 5 to 20 hours with the people who own each process. Open every interview with *"AI is not here to replace you, it's here to take the boring parts off your plate."* Scared employees give half answers, and half answers make useless audits.

**Then three plugin stages run over a materials folder** (documents, spreadsheets, email threads, auto-transcribed interviews):

1. **Extractor** — process review, findings review, waste review, citation-gated, consolidated into a map. (Demo output: 5 stages, 61 steps, 11 tools, 8 staff, 27 pain points, 13 waste items ≈ $1.08M/yr, plus contradictions, control gaps and follow-up questions.) It also generates the prep questions for your next interview.
2. **Researcher** — classifies their actual tool stack: which tools have open APIs, which have MCP connectors, which are locked. This is where recommendations stop being generic LinkedIn advice and become "your invoicing tool has an open API, here's the integration, 11 hours a week."
3. **Deliverable builder** — assembles the client-facing artifact: process maps, findings with source quotes, waste in dollars, the AI blueprint on an impact/effort matrix, and the **risk section** naming where AI should not go.

**The deliverable** is designed to be walked top to bottom on a call with a non-technical owner: a short education layer (the Excel analogy, amplification not replacement, why data centralization matters), then *how you work today · what we found · what can be optimized · hidden costs · your AI blueprint*. Every pain point carries the interview quote and links back to the recording. Every hidden-cost row uses a **role-specific hourly rate the client confirmed**, not a theoretical one.

**The three-layer explanation** — data → systems → intelligence — is what justifies the audit fee to anyone who wants to skip straight to "just build me the automation."

**About 80% of the deliverable is AI; the 20% is human review of every finding.** Knowing a sharp finding from a hallucinated one is the judgment you're actually paid for. End to end: **2–4 weeks.**

## 4 · The back end — where the money is

The audit is the entry point. Afterwards you know their processes better than most of their staff, you hold a prioritized build list with ROI on every line, and the client already agreed with the math because it came from their own team.

Five things to sell:
1. **Plugins and skills** — Claude-based SOPs; the easiest for staff to adopt, and most of what they sell.
2. **Training and workshops** — value scales with headcount.
3. **Small custom builds** — *small*, built **around** the trusted systems, because big companies can't risk downtime or a breach.
4. **Fully autonomous automations** — no human in the loop.
5. **Data migration** — when a closed legacy tool blocks everything else.

**The filter: does it save time or capture revenue — with a number attached?** If neither, don't sell it, even if the client asks.

**What a skill is:** inputs → **rules** → outputs. The value is entirely in the rules, because they live in the operator's head — the estimator applying 30 unwritten rules he's never articulated. Claude writes the file; **extracting the rules is the human job**, and it's the part nobody's nephew can do.

**The model:** transformation partner, not vendor. You teach them to build and edit their own skills. The retainer survives because transformation isn't a one-time event — the business changes, edge cases appear, new staff need training, and there's a stronger model and harness every few months. Openness is the positioning: *a partner who wants you independent is easier to trust than a vendor who needs you logged in.*

## 5 · The compounding claim

Every audit sharpens the plugins; every new industry adds patterns. **The moat isn't a skill file — it's the operating system you build around yourself and how fast it learns.** The proof, the pixel data, the plugins and the reputation all stack and none of them reset. Nothing here makes money in a weekend; the first client may take months and the first audit will be rough. Most people quit right before the compounding starts.

## What to notice before copying this

A few things are worth flagging, since the video is also an ad for the author's paid community:

- **The $85/booked-call and 80% call-two close rate are his numbers, in his market (Australia), with a two-year YouTube library behind the brand.** He says himself the paid channel is new and that ad leads show up *more* skeptical. Treat those figures as a target to test, not a plan input.
- **The audit is capital-intensive in time before it's profitable.** 5–20 hours of interviews plus review, at a $3k first price, is not a high hourly rate. The economics only work once the plugins are built and the back end sells.
- **"Hundreds of hours went into the audit plugin."** The plugins are the actual asset, and they're what he's selling access to. Building your own version is the real project, not the funnel.
- **$100/day ad spend before anything closes is real money out of pocket.** His own sequencing advice — prove the offer with outreach and inbound first — is the important part.

## The order to actually run this

1. Pick the niche and write the one-line ICP callout.
2. Define the audit offer, the guarantee, and your honest starting price.
3. Build the demo audit (dummy data, real structure) — it's the sales asset *and* the delivery spec.
4. Get 2–3 audits at the floor price through outreach and your existing network. Record the testimonials at the peak.
5. Turn what you did by hand into the extractor → researcher → builder skills, one at a time.
6. Stand up the funnel: landing page, name-and-phone-first capture, speed-to-lead, confirmation call.
7. Only then turn on paid, to scale a formula that already works.
8. Sell the back end into the map you already hold.
