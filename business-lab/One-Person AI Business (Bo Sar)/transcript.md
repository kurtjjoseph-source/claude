## 00:00 — Why the standard AI-agency playbook stopped working

There are hour-long courses all over YouTube teaching the same thing: pick a niche, build a chatbot demo, send a hundred cold emails a day, apply to Upwork jobs, wait for a reply. That's roughly how I would have taught it a year ago — and I did teach some of it on this channel. A year ago it worked.

What changed is scale. Those courses have pulled in millions of views over two years. When millions of people download the same playbook, it stops being a playbook and becomes noise. Every business owner you email has already had forty emails exactly like yours this week, from people who watched the same videos you did.

So let me set expectations. I'm assuming you're not looking for a shortcut, you're not chasing a get-rich-quick scheme, and you already know what a chatbot is. You want to know how this business actually works in 2026, when anyone can build the basics and every prospect is tired of AI pitches. I'll walk you through the exact system our agency runs end to end: the offer, the funnel behind our ads, how the sales calls go word for word, the $10,000 deliverable itself on screen, and our numbers including the uncomfortable ones.

And by the end you'll see the most interesting part: one person can run this entire machine without 14-hour days, because the agency becomes the first client of its own AI.

## 01:31 — The commodity trap and the trust gap

Eighteen months ago, if you walked into a construction company and showed them an AI system that answers quote requests automatically, you looked like a wizard. Today the owner's nephew builds the same thing in a weekend with a ChatGPT subscription. The demo that used to impress a dental clinic is now a prompt away for anybody.

**That's the commodity trap.** The ability to build was the moat. That moat is gone.

Then there's the paradox that confuses everyone: demand has never been higher. Businesses aren't skeptical about AI any more — they're past that. They know they need it and they're scared of being left behind. They're also drowning in AI noise; their feeds, their competitors, even their kids talk about it. So *starting* a conversation with a business owner about AI is the easiest it has ever been. *Closing* that conversation is the hardest it has ever been.

The technology adoption curve explains it. Every new technology gets bought first by early adopters — risk-takers who jump on unknown things just to be first. Much later, the majority arrives. Two years ago I was showing this exact curve on this channel and saying: right now you're selling to early adopters, which is an easy sell, so build your portfolio of case studies *now*, because when the majority shows up they won't throw money at something just because it looks impressive. They'll want proof and a clear return.

The majority has arrived. That's who you're selling to today — careful buyers who have seen enough AI hype. **The real gap in this market is trust, not awareness.**

We're living it. For two years our agency built custom solutions — chatbots, process automations, voice agents, full MVPs for SaaS founders — and every single lead came from this YouTube channel. A YouTube lead arrives with trust already built. Think about what happens before the call: someone watches a 40-minute video where I explain the exact thing they need, watches me build it on screen for another client, and by the time they book they've educated themselves, they know what they want, and they've already decided I'm the person to do it. The call is half closed before it starts.

Recently we launched Meta ads, because we want a system that doesn't depend on my face and this channel. Getting clicks was the easy part — everybody wants AI right now, everybody is scared of falling behind. People click the ad, watch the video on the landing page, book the call. But they show up skeptical, and you can't blame them: they met you thirty seconds ago in their feed. An ad and a landing page video cannot do what a two-year YouTube library does.

Awareness is identical. What's missing is trust. Which means you have to bring the proof into the call — things they can see, numbers they can check, clients who look like them.

## 05:21 — Not all proof counts

Everyone will tell you the answer is to build proof and get case studies. That's correct but incomplete, because **not all proof counts.**

If you get lucky and close a random chatbot project because you found the one business owner who couldn't build it himself — congratulations, you made a few thousand dollars. That is not proof for a serious company. A business doing three, five, ten million a year, looking for someone to help them transform how they operate, could not care less about your chatbot. There's no story in it. I know, because we got a ton of those projects.

Real proof looks like this: *before we came in, their quoting process took the team 20 hours a week; now it takes three; that's $28,000 a year in one process — and here's the client saying it on camera.* A real before and after, with tangible ROI, from a client who looks exactly like the next client you want.

That kind of proof doesn't come from random gigs. It comes from a specific kind of engagement with a specific kind of client. Which raises the question the basic courses never ask: **how do you consistently get the kind of client whose project produces that case study?**

Everything that follows — the offer, the funnel, the sales system, the delivery — is one machine designed to do two things: convert proof into revenue, and convert that revenue back into more proof.

## 07:41 — The map: traffic, system, skill

Any service business — an agency, a consultancy, a law firm, a plumbing company — comes down to three elements.

- **Traffic** fills your funnel with opportunities.
- **System** converts those opportunities into appointments, contracts and cash.
- **Skill** delivers the work so well that you get paid, kept and referred.

Why is that useful and not just a pretty triangle? Because at any moment your business has exactly **one binding constraint**, and running a business is just solving constraints one after another. You have a traffic problem, a system problem, or a skill problem — not all three at once. One of them is the bottleneck, and working on the other two while ignoring it is how people stay stuck for years.

Almost everyone starting an AI business gets this wrong the same way: they obsess over skill — cooler demos, another tool, another certification — while their actual constraint is traffic and trust. They can build, but nobody knows them.

**One more rule that will save you months: one traffic source, one conversion mechanism, one delivery channel.** Don't stack five channels before one works end to end. Build the plumbing first, then send water through the pipes. When something breaks — and something always breaks — you know exactly which of the three pieces to fix, because there's only one of each.

## 09:13 — The 2026 reframe: AI-first

2026 changes this framework in one specific place, and the whole video hangs on it.

In the classic version you scale by **transferring skill into people**. The owner is the best salesperson and the best deliverer, so growth means hiring and teaching them to do what the owner does — slowly, expensively, with constant turnover.

In the AI-first version you don't transfer the skill to employees. You transfer it into Claude — into plugins and skills that carry your delivery process, your proposal process, your project management. **The skill stops living in your head and starts living in files.** Just markdown files.

That's exactly why a one-person AI business is possible today and wasn't three years ago. Nothing changed about motivation or hard work. What changed is that scaling used to mean hiring and training people, and now the skill lives in files you can copy for free.

## 10:46 — Traffic: the four realistic sources

You have four realistic ways to fill a funnel, and each has trade-offs. Every course sells you exactly one of them as *the* way and conveniently skips its downsides.

**1. Outreach — Upwork, LinkedIn, cold email.** This is where about 90% of the basic courses live. Outreach is fine for your very first clients: it gets you reps and real conversations. If you've never sold anything, go take some Upwork calls — it's instant practice. But understand what it is. It's the freelancer path: you show up with zero trust, you compete on price against the whole planet, and every deal starts from scratch. You finish a project and you're unemployed again. It's a treadmill, not a machine. And in 2026 response rates for AI services are brutal, because forty other people watched the same course you did.

**2. Content — the inbound path.** For two years this channel brought us 90% of our clients, with trust built before the call started. Inbound leads are about the best leads that exist, because you're not a stranger — you're the expert who has been breaking things down for them. But you're not in control: the algorithm decides your month. It's unpredictable, it takes years to compound, and it's tied to your face. You can't hire someone to be you. If you want a company that runs without your face on every asset, content alone can't be the engine.

**3. Paid ads.** What makes paid different fits in one sentence: **paid traffic is the only source where volume responds to money, on demand.** If your unit economics work — a client is worth more than a client costs — you control both volume and timing. Spend more this week, get more calls booked next week. It's a dial. And the moment the dial works, your constraint stops being traffic and moves to system and skill, which is a fantastic trade, because those are things you can engineer.

**4. Referrals and reputation.** I put it last on purpose: it's the best one, and you can't start with it. Referred leads are the cheapest, highest-trust leads in existence. Most people miss that **referrals are a system, not luck**:
- Ask at the peak — right after you deliver value, never during a lull.
- Ask with a specific list. "Do you know any electricians? Any landscaping companies?" beats the vague "know anyone who needs this?"
- Pay 20% commission on referred revenue, gladly.
- Price so the client gets roughly 5× the value they paid. Underpriced value is what makes clients talk about you.

The catch: referrals multiply your client base, and when you have three clients even a perfect referral system is a trickle. It's a compounding multiplier on great delivery, not a dial you can turn when you're small.

**How they fit together.** Outreach gets you your first conversations. Content slowly earns trust in the background. Paid gives you control over how many leads come in. And when you deliver well, referrals multiply everything on top. Delivery is the piece that connects it all.

## 14:38 — General traffic brings general leads

One lesson from two years of content, and it's an expensive one: **general traffic brings general leads.** My videos brought every request you can imagine — a logistics company wants a dashboard, a coach wants a funnel, a manufacturer wants who knows what. Every client a different task. That's an outsourcing treadmill, and nothing about it is repeatable.

The moment you call out a specific ICP in your traffic, everything changes. Pick a niche — say construction — and let your ad literally open with *"construction companies above $1 million a year."* Now you attract construction companies with the same problems: quoting, scheduling, invoicing, all leaking hours in the same places.

Similar problems → you can template the solution → templated solutions → you can productize → productized delivery is the thing you can automate.

That single shift — calling your buyer out by name in the traffic itself — is what turns gigs into a business.

## 16:08 — The paid traffic doctrine

Here's our entire Meta ads doctrine in five minutes. If you've watched media-buying tutorials, this will sound too simple.

**The setup:** one campaign, a single ad set, around 20 different creatives inside it, campaign budget optimization, about $100/day to start.

**Targeting is just the country** (Australia in our case). We don't touch age ranges, genders or interests at all. A few years ago every tutorial said the opposite — I watched them, I ran those campaigns. But Meta in 2026 works like this: your targeting settings are suggestions, and Meta can ignore them. The algorithm watches who actually converts and reallocates by itself. We watched it happen: with zero age settings, Meta quietly poured budget into 45–54-year-olds — exactly our buyer, business owners with real companies — while spending almost nothing on 18-year-olds. Nobody told it to. The pixel figured it out.

**So what do you control? The creatives.** 95% of your results come from them: different hooks, different formats, some video, some static. Let the algorithm find the winners. Forget trying to out-target Meta; your job is to keep feeding it new creatives to test.

**You niche down through the message, not the settings.** The first line of your ad copy says who it's for — *"Australian businesses making above $1 million a year."* 90% of people never read past the first line, so the first line does the qualifying. And when the wrong people see the ad and bounce off the page, that's good: the bounce teaches the pixel who *not* to bring next time. Wrong-fit traffic leaving is a feature.

Optimize for booked calls, and make sure your Conversions API is set up correctly.

**The numbers.** Our benchmark is around **$85 per qualified booked call**. Napkin math: spend $4,000 a week → roughly 50 booked calls → close 20–30% of a $10,000 offer → you're looking at a million-dollar business run by a very small team.

And yes, this needs budget. $100 a day is real money when nothing has closed yet, out of your own pocket. Which is exactly why the order matters: **outreach and inbound first, until the offer is proven and a few clients have paid — then paid traffic, to scale a formula that already works, not to discover one.** Discovering one with paid is too expensive.

## 19:15 — The funnel

What happens between the click and a booked call is the part no course teaches, because those courses have never run a funnel.

**The landing page** is deliberately boring technology — plain HTML, no fancy framework, because frameworks load slower and every second of load time costs you leads. The page has a video sales letter (VSL) explaining the offer, plus testimonials and case studies. Below all of that come the qualifying questions and the calendar.

Nobody is forced to watch the video — anyone can scroll down and book right away. The real filtering happens in the qualifying questions before the calendar. The video's job is different: the people who *do* watch arrive knowing what the offer is, what it costs to ignore the problem, and who we are. In practice serious buyers usually watch it, because it sells.

**The trick that pays for the entire tool stack** — and almost nobody knows it exists — is the booking flow. It starts with just a **name and phone number**. The moment someone enters those, a record is created in our CRM instantly. Everything after that — the qualifying questions about headcount, revenue, their bottleneck, and the calendar itself — comes as separate steps.

So when somebody gets distracted halfway through, or changes their mind and closes the tab before picking a slot, most funnels lose that person forever. We already have their name and number. A notification fires instantly and **we call them within 5 to 10 minutes** — not tomorrow, not within one business day. You call while your page is still open in their brain.

My favourite real example: we called a lead who abandoned the form, and the guy said *"oh, I have ADHD, I got distracted, thank you for reminding me"* — and booked on the spot, on the phone. That's a client the ads already paid for who would otherwise have disappeared. **Speed to lead is a superpower and it's free.**

(The booking system we use for this — capturing name and phone before everything else — is called Iclose. Not affiliated, it's just a really good tool for optimising bookings.)

## 21:31 — Operational rules

**Confirmation call.** When someone books, our closer still calls them within five minutes. That one short call does three jobs at once:
1. They commit, because they just told a real person "yes, I'll be there" — and people show up for promises they said out loud.
2. We tell them to watch one of my videos while they wait, so they arrive already understanding what we do in depth.
3. That same video builds the trust an ad never could — it shrinks the trust gap by placing long-form trust-building between the booking and the call. People book one day ahead and still forget, and every no-show costs you the ad spend that brought it.

**No-shows: call immediately and double dial** — two calls back to back. Some phones treat a repeated call within a few minutes as urgent and let it ring through silent mode. But the bigger reason is simpler: one missed call from an unknown number is easy to ignore as spam; a second call right after signals a real person who actually needs you. People pick up the second one far more often.

Keep it casual — *"hey, I was waiting for you, where are you?"* lands better than any formal follow-up email. Don't be afraid to call people: they filled out your form because they wanted this. About 90% of people who pick up say some version of "sorry, I forgot," and rebook on the spot.

**When you rebook, never send a link.** Offer exactly two options: *"I can do tomorrow at 10, or the day after at 5."* Don't let them think. If they say "I'll book after dinner" — no, give two options and book it for them. An open calendar is a decision; two slots is a coin flip.

**One practical note:** if you're selling into a different country (we sell into Australia), a simple caller app on Twilio lets you dial out from local numbers in your target market so your calls don't look foreign. That's an afternoon build with Claude Code, and it makes the whole speed-to-lead system work from anywhere in the world.

None of this is glamorous — which is exactly why it works and why almost nobody does it. The gap between a funnel that *works* and a funnel that books qualified calls every day is a stack of boring operational habits like these.

## 24:38 — Sales call one

This is where most technical people fall apart. Two calls, each with one job.

**Call one's goal is not closing. Its goal is a scheduled call two.** We're selling a higher-ticket offer, and everything in call one builds towards that.

**You talk 20% of the time, the client talks 80%. You're there to diagnose, not to pitch.** Which department is leaking the most time? What does the quoting process look like? Who does the invoicing? How many people touch onboarding? What tools is all of this running on?

**Dig for numbers, always.** When the owner says "our estimating takes forever," don't nod and move on. Ask how many hours "forever" actually is, who does it, and what their hourly cost is. **Hours × rate = hidden cost, and hidden cost is the entire foundation of the deal.** If a process takes 20 hours a week across four people, that's a number — and numbers close deals, vague adjectives don't.

**My favourite move in the entire sales process, and it's counterintuitive: somewhere in that call, tell them where AI *shouldn't* be used in their business.** Everyone pitching this owner is hype — every consultant, every agency, every ad says AI everything, AI everywhere. So when you're the one who says *"this process I wouldn't touch with AI, the data is too sensitive and the failure cost is too high,"* something changes in the room. A 20-year operator can smell hype a mile away, and the realist backed by experience wins instantly. **Trust is built by what you refuse to sell.**

**Then, at the end of call one, you show the deliverable.** This might be our most powerful sales asset, because they can *see* it. It's a demo audit — dummy data, real structure — and we walk the prospect through it live. What's in it:
- a process map of how a company like theirs actually operates, department by department;
- pain points, each tied to an AI solution;
- a hidden cost table where **every number quotes the client's own team members**, so nobody can say we invented the math (that's why we run the interviews);
- a priority matrix — what to build first, impact versus effort;
- a risk section: where AI should *not* go.

The prospect needs to see the thing to buy the thing. An audit is invisible; this makes it physical.

**Right there, while they're looking at it, you name the price** — $10,000, or whatever yours is — **together with the guarantee.** Ours: if the audit doesn't find at least $50,000 a year in recoverable waste, they get a full refund.

**Then you book call two before they leave the call**, live, into the calendar, and you ask them to click the confirmation while you watch: *"can you click yes on that confirmation email? Perfect, thank you."* It sounds tiny. It isn't — confirmed calls happen at a much higher rate.

## 28:26 — The proposal, 15 minutes later

Fifteen minutes after call one ends, there's a proposal in their inbox. Not a template with their logo slapped on — a professional, personalised document that we went through many iterations to make sure doesn't read like AI slop. It quotes their exact words from the call, and covers the problem, the solution, exactly what we're going to do, and specific pricing with the ROI explained in their own numbers.

**How it runs.** I use Claude Code (inside Antigravity — that's just my habit; the same skill works in the Claude desktop app). I type `proposal` and the client's name. It automatically pulls the discovery-call transcript from my AI note-taker (Fathom), which syncs into my Obsidian vault, which Claude Code has access to.

> This is important: **have a centralized place where you gather all your data, legible and available to your AI brain.** In my case Obsidian is the business brain and Claude pulls everything from it.

It finds the transcript, sets up the client folder, fetches their website, reads the skill template and references, and comes back with a proposal plan. I skim it, approve the flagged decisions (it asked me to confirm the guarantee figure — I said $50k — and took its own suggestions for the rest), and it generates a ready-to-send PDF, an HTML file, and the email draft.

Two details worth noticing:

- **It's proactive.** Unprompted, it said it would check my calendar for a booked follow-up with this client before writing the closing line — so if a meeting is already scheduled, the proposal ends with "see you Thursday as agreed." I never told it to do that.
- **There's a second agent doing an unbiased, skeptical proposal review**, a few iterations, to get it to actually match my quality template. And before drafting the send email it checks past correspondence with that lead to match the tone and style of the existing thread.

The whole run takes about five minutes. The email draft reads well — no em dashes, no "I hope you're doing well," no slop. And you can connect Claude Code to Gmail over MCP and just say "send it."

**The proposal template** (our current production one) is titled *"Where the money leaks and what to build first"*:
- their problem areas, 1–4, exactly what was discussed on the call;
- **your solution** — process audit, data and tools audit, existing automations review, AI transformation roadmap, first automation;
- **why us** — the proof, with clickable links;
- **the roadmap** — very specific steps so the client has clarity: blueprint delivered → review and prioritisation → first build → training, with what happens at each milestone;
- **your investment** — 50% upfront, 50% on delivery, total for the audit;
- **the guarantee** — at least $50,000/year in recoverable waste found, or a full refund. A no-brainer.

Speed is not the moat — every AI agency can send an AI-generated proposal 15 minutes after a call. The value is in all those iterations that make it sound professional and real rather than AI slop. That's the balance: not an elaborate AI-generated UI, and not a bare email saying "we'll do this, pay us that."

## 35:24 — Sales call two

They show up, usually with a business partner or whoever is more technical — a CTO. **If they show up, this closes at around 80%.** The heavy lifting already happened: they've seen the deliverable, the price is anchored, and the guarantee should have killed the risk.

So call two handles the remaining questions and logistics — payment terms. Ours: 50% upfront, 50% on completion. The first half clears before any delivery starts.

**And one rule we never break: the sale is a sale only when the money is in the bank** — not when they say yes. People promise you the world on calls, and very often it doesn't happen.

## 36:56 — The one real objection

There's really only one big objection. Here's how it happened recently.

A prospect, between call one and call two, got approached by another AI agency. Their pitch: *why pay $10,000 for an audit when we'll just build you the automation you want for $6,000 — cheaper, faster, no report, the real build right away?*

Our answer, almost word for word: **that agency doesn't understand your whole business.** They'll automate one part, and that part isn't built around everything else — so the process next to it starts leaking, then the one after that, and you'll be plugging hole after hole until one day you pay someone to rip it all out and rebuild it properly. Which costs more than mapping the business first and building the right things in the right order.

The client stayed, because the logic is real. You wouldn't build a house without a foundation, and the map is the foundation. More experienced business owners understand this.

**There's a pattern worth knowing:** the prospects who push hardest to skip the audit are almost always small — five to ten person teams where the owner is sure he knows everything happening in his business. Decision makers at bigger companies never argue with the audit; they know they don't know. Treat it as a qualification signal, and be willing to walk away.

## 38:30 — Selling with zero case studies

How do you sell this with no proof? **You buy your proof — by charging less.**

Your first audits might not be $10,000. They might be $3,000. But you **never** say "you're one of our first clients, so it's cheaper." You don't announce that you're new or inexperienced. You simply price lower, so the barrier to entry is lower and saying yes is easier.

**Don't go too low either. $3,000 is the floor** — for a business above a million a year, a suspiciously cheap audit reads as a red flag, not a deal. If you're really fresh, anchor at $5,000 and let it come down to $3,000 during a negotiation, live on the follow-up call — not as a scripted discount story.

Early buyers literally asked us, years ago, "how are you even making money on this?" That's exactly the reaction you want, because you're not making money yet. **Your goal is to collect the before-and-after stories** — the real case studies that let you charge $10,000 for an audit later.

Same with the testimonial: you don't state it up front as a condition of the discount. You ask **at the peak**, right after you deliver value, when the client is excited. Then record it as an interview, where you control the questions so you can extract all the value you delivered.

**A variation we're testing, which you can steal:** if prospects hesitate to pay for what sounds like "just a report," **bundle the first automation into the audit price.** They don't just get the blueprint — they get the first item on the priority matrix actually built. The deliverable stops being paper and becomes a before-and-after, which is exactly what you need most at the beginning. Easier to sell, and it produces a case study the moment the audit is done.

## 40:49 — Delivering the audit

**The raw material of an audit is interviews.** This is the most time-consuming part. You get on calls with the people who actually own each process — the operations lead, the estimator, the office manager — depending on the company and industry. Depending on company size that's somewhere between **5 and 20 hours of real conversation** with their employees.

You ask: what does your day look like? Where does the time go? What tools are you fighting with? What do you do when something goes wrong? (Our plugin also prepares you for the interviews.)

**A tip from experience: before you start any interview with a new employee, open with the line that AI is not here to replace you — it's here to take the boring parts off your plate.** Say it in the first five minutes, because it's true. Some employees are scared, scared employees give half answers, and half answers make useless audits.

## 42:23 — The audit plugin, on screen

First, the **materials folder** — everything the client has shared: documents, spreadsheets they use, relevant email threads, and every meeting and interview, automatically transcribed and stored. That's the foundation all the skills work from.

**Skill 1 — the extractor.** I run *"run the extractor pipeline for Ironbark Fencing"* (anonymised client). It extracts all materials, transcripts and threads, then runs a process review, a findings review and a waste review, and gives me a consolidated report.

It took **several hours** — and that's only the first skill. There are so many tasks and revisions underneath. We put hundreds of hours into this audit plugin, revising and iterating every one of them to get output at this quality.

With only three meetings and three emails injected for the demo, the output was: 3 transcripts extracted (kickoff, estimating, ops and admin), each validated through a citation gate and merged sequentially; 3 email threads, all context-relevant; a process review with 9 BPMN sub-agents and cross-cutting checks; a findings review identifying **27 pain points**; and a waste review with **13 waste items** verified against transcripts.

The final map: **5 stages, 61 steps, 11 tools, 8 staff, 40 business metrics, 27 pain points, 13 waste items ≈ $1.08M/year gross** — plus 4 contradictions, 5 control gaps and 23 follow-up questions. Status stays *in progress*, not complete, because that's your call.

**It also preps your next interview.** I typed *"generate follow-up questions for tomorrow's session with Wayne Duffy, the site supervisor"* and got a file: what we covered last session (inquiries, quoting, job handover, office finance across three sessions), what we're covering today (on-site variations), and the questions themselves — *"walk me through a typical month of on-site variations: roughly how many extra-work jobs come up, what's a normal dollar range, and honestly how many end up invoiced versus quietly done for free"* — with a pointer explaining that this unlocks the single biggest site number: work done on site that never gets billed.

**Skill 2 — the researcher.** *"audit improvement researcher"* researches the client's tool stack: which tools have APIs, which have MCP connectors, what can actually be automated versus what's locked inside a closed system. **This is where recommendations stop being generic.** Not "use AI for your invoicing" like a random LinkedIn post, but: your invoicing tool has an open API, here's the exact integration, and it saves 11 hours a week. Real consulting based on real evaluation of their real stack.

A few more hours later: **11 tools classified** (open API vs closed, no API), **17 proposed changes** grouped into three plugin bundles and rated with honest quantified value per year, and a **three-phase blueprint** — quick wins with numbers attached, core builds, future sprints.

**Skill 3 — the deliverable builder.** *"audit deliverable builder"* assembles the deliverable itself: process maps, findings with their source quotes, waste quantified in dollars, the AI blueprint with every initiative placed on an impact-versus-effort priority matrix, and the risk section — the places where we tell the client *not* to use AI, which by now you understand is a feature, not a disclaimer.

## 49:19 — Walking a client through the deliverable

## 63:58 — What a skill actually is

When we say "we build AI plugins," it sounds like magic until you see the shape of one. A skill is a documented SOP with three parts:

- **Inputs** — the information that goes in.
- **Rules** — what happens to that information along the way.
- **Outputs** — what comes out and where it lands.

Take a proposal skill for a construction company. Inputs: client name, project details, line items. Rules: which template to use, how the pricing tiers work, and that any deal over $5,000 gets the extended terms section. Output: a finished PDF landing in the client's document system.

**The whole value hides in the middle.** Anyone can name the inputs and the outputs. The rules are the hard part, because they live in the operator's head. The estimator who's been quoting jobs for 15 years doesn't know he's applying 30 small rules every time — he just does it.

**So your job in the audit interviews is to surface the rules that people don't know they follow.** Claude writes the skill file; extracting that knowledge is on you. And that's the part nobody's nephew with a ChatGPT subscription can do, because it takes real expertise to know which questions to ask and how to map the processes.
