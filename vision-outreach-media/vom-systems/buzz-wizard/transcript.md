Jack Dorsey just launched Buzz and it's gone completely viral. Yes, it's the same Jack Dorsey who co-founded Twitter, who co-founded Block. This guy is building what people are calling a Slack killer. It's being called a Slack killer because it's almost like this agentic version of Slack. It's a way to have conversations with your teammates except their agents. It's a way for you to huddle agents. It's a way for you to have software being built on the fly via agent. In this episode we break it down so you understand what it is, why you should use it, and the best tips and tricks. My take: it's a glimpse into the future of work.

**What will people learn?** How to use Buzz. The sweet spot right now is solopreneurs and small teams — brainstorm, keep everything in context between agents, build products, build solutions, ideate, get stuff out there quicker and be more productive.

**The real selling point — openness.** Buzz is Slack with agents inside. Slack lets you add agents via integration; in Buzz they're *first-class citizens* — members of your team, an integral part of the app. You get default agents when you start, but under the surface you can edit and add agents. Giving an agent instructions is basically a system prompt. The really powerful thing underneath is the **harness** — it's swappable under any agent. You can use Claude Code, Codex, Goose, Open Code. AI moves at a breakneck pace and people get "model fatigue"; Buzz is a layer on top so you can just switch. And you don't lose anything in the switch — all the context in your chats comes with you to the new harness / new model. Skills and skill files work as normal as long as they're installed globally (not project-specific) so Buzz can access them.

**Audio huddles with agents.** Have a voice chat with your agents; they get the context of your whole chat in the huddle. You can have a voice call with someone else, bring an agent in, and it responds. Greg's dream: eventually FaceTime agents — the holy grail of the future of work. Buzz is scratching the surface.

**Git + projects.** Agents are tightly integrated with git. They create projects, create feature branches, and work in **parallel work trees** (copies) so they don't mess with what's locally on your computer — they can work in parallel. Example: talking about a UI in chat, tell the agent "spin up three different versions of landing pages based on the ideas we had." There's a new Projects view (turn on the experiment in settings — workflows and projects). You can integrate with GitHub, but under the surface Buzz has its own hosted git on your **relay**. Relays are servers hosted by you or for you by Buzz/Block — where all your chats, information, and pushes live. They're taking on more than Slack — GitHub too.

**Building on the fly.** Example: "spin up a simple CRM app using the Wasp full-stack framework and deploy it to Railway for me." It did it — pushed to remote repositories, deployed live on the web, even sent screenshots and preview links, before he checked what it was doing. A lot of workflows and prompts are baked into the agents so they work end-to-end. Then you add teammates to the chat, discuss missing features / what you don't like, and tell the agents to start working on it. You can create software on the fly, automated, high-quality, deployable, in a clear managed view.

**The closed context loop (the "boring stuff" edge).** Vinnie built a tweet leaderboard full-stack app (how much the team tweets, top tweets, stats). Then he had Buzz *also access the app* via a **publicly-facing API** + a **workflow**: daily, check the API and post the numbers back into the channel. Now the agents have that data in context — he can reply "Fizz, what's the common thread between my successful tweets this last 7 days?" and get analysis, brainstorm new angles. Information goes in, comes out through apps, and it all lands in the agents' context. Openness makes these integrations easy — with Slack you'd need API tokens, create an app, learn proprietary structure; Buzz is built on open protocols so it's fast. "Novelty gets you reach, friction gets you engagement" — real advice that surfaced from the loop.

**Talking to agents.** No special approach — they're good at pulling intent. Have a normal conversation, be straightforward and concise.

**Bitcoin-native payments (backdoor).** Buzz is built on **Nostr**, an open protocol tightly coupled with **Bitcoin Lightning** (fast, near-feeless). Jack Dorsey is a big Bitcoin fan. The guess: you'll be able to integrate Lightning payments — agents pay for compute (shared compute idea), agents pay for work, or people tip each other for tasks (e.g., tip a designer in a Buzz community for a logo). Not integrated yet, but likely coming.

**Shared compute / local models.** A compute setting suggests local LLMs you can download and *share with your team*. Example: college students without money for a Max Pro plan pool together, buy a beefy Mac Mini / Mac Studio, share compute, and everyone uses that one model from different laptops. They're paving the road for real openness in model choice and harnessing local/open-source models that are catching up to frontier models. Why local matters: model choice, and **data control**. Slack controls your data and it's hard/impossible to take it elsewhere — same for models. Prices could jump ($200/mo today, who knows tomorrow); some models have usage restrictions or government mandates; models get pulled. You're building your business on these tools, so you should have flexibility, freedom, and control.

**The big insight.** We didn't realize how much of a **context hub** Slack became. To get the best out of models you need the most context possible. Buzz recognizes your chat as your *context foundation* and helps you pivot in many directions (GitHub projects, shared compute, huddles) — but whatever you do, **the context is at the core.** You expand your "team" (including agents) and make your global context window available to everyone on the team.

**Managing agents — the sauce.** You probably don't need to do much. First thing Vinnie did: **pin agents to models** — Fizz = Fable, Honey = Sonnet — because some tasks don't need the power of the big model and burn tokens fast. He made a "Chief Agent Officer" to *delegate the delegation*: "I have this task, who's best for it?" (copywriter, brainstormer, code reviewer, etc.). Skills installed globally are available to Buzz agents.

**Reality check.** It's beta (maybe alpha) — early preview software. Workflows / recurring tasks weren't landing great. It can be slow (it talks to your relay and back) vs. working directly in Claude Code / Codex. Good for solopreneurs and small teams iterating on small software/ideas; for complex software engineering it may not be the tool yet — but if you want one central tool that brings all your knowledge together, it's great.

**Who should use it.** Founders / small teams (1–10 people, $0–10M revenue) on Slack today should download Buzz and try it. Vinnie is trying to get his Wasp team to use it internally. You can create **private and public channels** — invite your community into public channels, keep private channels for your team, and the context is shared. If someone reports a bug in your community channel, fixing it with an agent is "an @ away" — "Fizz, let's tackle this bug."

**Closing take.** It's scratching the surface of the future of work — a world with more agent employees than teammates. It feels early. Greg (co-founder of LCA, Late Checkout Agency) sees it's close but not there. Worth trying regardless — you'll learn something, even if you just end up configuring Slack better, or you fall in love with Buzz. In the agentic era the most important thing is getting your hands dirty, learning how these tools work, playing with them, seeing how you'd live in the future. It's open-source, built on an open protocol — anyone can improve it, the hive mind works on it, and it might become the default. But things change fast, so who knows.
